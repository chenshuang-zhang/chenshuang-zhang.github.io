SVHalluc: Benchmarking Speech–Vision Hallucination in Audio-Visual Large Language Models

Chenshuang Zhang, Kyeong Seon Kim, Chengxin Liu, Tae-Hyun Oh
KAIST
TL;DR: SVHalluc is the first benchmark for speech-induced hallucinations in audio-visual LLMs, revealing failures in grounding speech content in visual evidence.
Paper Code Data Poster Slides

Motivation: Speech Content ≠ Visual Evidence

Existing audio-visual hallucination studies mainly test whether environmental sound events are visually present. SVHalluc studies a more challenging setting: speech can mention objects, actions, and temporal references that are not supported by the current video.

Unlike environmental sounds, speech carries rich semantic and temporal content. Models should not treat what is said as what is seen.

Prior benchmarks

Sound event hallucination

Prior work often asks whether a heard environmental sound, such as a cat meowing, corresponds to a visible event.

Speech-induced hallucination

Speech can mention non-visible content. For example, a model may hear “egg” and hallucinate that an egg is visible.

New challenge

Ground speech in vision

SVHalluc evaluates whether AV-LLMs can verify spoken content against visual evidence, rather than simply relying on the transcript.

Abstract

Unlike environmental sounds that mainly indicate event occurrence, human speech carries rich semantics and temporal structures. Despite the advancement of audio-visual large language models in video understanding, it remains underexplored whether current models can accurately align speech content with corresponding visual signals. In this work, we show that speech content can induce hallucinations in audio-visual LLMs, where models generate inaccurate or misleading outputs by confusing what is said with what is visually supported.

We introduce SVHalluc, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating speech–vision hallucination in audio-visual LLMs. SVHalluc diagnoses hallucinations from two complementary perspectives: semantic grounding, which asks what speech content is visible, and temporal grounding, which asks when narrated actions visually occur. Experimental results show that current audio-visual LLMs still struggle to ground speech in vision, revealing a fundamental limitation of cross-modal speech-video understanding.

SVHalluc Benchmark

SVHalluc contains six diagnostic tasks organized around two questions: what speech content is visually supported, and when narrated events occur visually.

Semantic Tasks

What speech content is visible?

GSA
Global Semantic AlignmentVideo-level semantic matching.
FGSA
Fine-Grained Semantic AlignmentObject-level semantic grounding.
CMSB
Cross-Modal Semantic BindingEvent-level semantic matching.

Temporal Tasks

When does the narrated action visually occur?

TA
Temporal AlignmentSame-time speech-video matching.
TF
Temporal ForecastingPast / present / future reasoning.
CMTB
Cross-Modal Temporal BindingDistinguishing speech from the current video.

Six tasks jointly evaluate whether models can align speech content with visual evidence at semantic and temporal levels.

Task Examples

Models should not assume narrated content is visible, and should not assume narrated events are happening at the current moment.

Global Semantic Alignment
Speech: “Pour out the water.”
Question: Does the speech describe the visual events in the video?
Options: (A) Yes   (B) No
Answer: depends on whether the video globally matches the spoken description.
Fine-Grained Semantic Alignment
Speech: “I’m going to place the first slice of bread in.”
Question: Is the slice of bread visible in the video?
Options: (A) Yes   (B) No
Answer: models must check visual evidence instead of relying only on the transcript.
Cross-Modal Semantic Binding
Speech: “Now what I’m going to do is lay the cheddar cheese over the top.”
Question: Is the following event visible in the video: cook cheddar cheese until it becomes soft?
Options: (A) Yes   (B) No
Answer: models must bind the spoken event to the visual event, not just detect related words.
Temporal Alignment
Speech: “Bag with about a third of a cup of flour.”
Question: When the speech is heard, is the narrated event happening at the same time in the video?
Options: (A) Yes   (B) No
Answer: models must align the speech moment with the visual timeline.
Temporal Forecasting
Speech: “Bag the meat with flour.”
Question: Relative to the moment when the speech is heard, when does the narrated event occur in the video?
Options: (A) Past   (B) Present   (C) Future
Answer: models must reason whether the event is past, present, or future relative to speech.
Cross-Modal Temporal Binding
Speech: “Each cube or a bunch? I would do it with several at a time.”
Question: When the speech is heard, what visual action is occurring in the video?
Options: (A) put seasoned meat in a zip lock bag with flour   (B) add herbs and wine   (C) season diced beef with salt and pepper
Answer: models must identify the current visual action rather than repeat nearby speech content.

Main Results

Current audio-visual LLMs still struggle with speech–vision grounding, even when they can process both video and audio inputs.

1

AV-LLMs perform poorly on SVHalluc

Open-source models perform near random on multiple tasks. Commercial models perform better, but still hallucinate.

2

Global alignment is difficult

Models can be better at object-level grounding, but still fail to judge whether speech globally matches the video.

3

Temporal grounding remains challenging

Models often confuse narrated events with what is happening visually at the current speech moment.

Suggested figures: overall semantic/temporal accuracy and task-wise gain over random chance.

Why Do Models Fail?

Our analysis suggests that speech–vision hallucination is not merely a speech perception problem. The key bottleneck is cross-modal integration.

Speech perception is strong

Models can recognize most speech content and localize when speech is heard.

WER: 0.09 ↓ mIoU: 0.88 ↑

Cross-modal integration is weak

Adding transcripts helps some tasks, but does not solve the core alignment problem. Models still struggle to decide whether the narrated content is visually supported.

Transcript ≠ grounding Speech ≠ evidence
Strong speech perception does not guarantee reliable speech–vision integration.
